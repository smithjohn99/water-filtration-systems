Why filter your tap water at all?

My tap water was technically fine. That's what the city report said every year. But it tasted like a swimming pool, my kettle scaled up every few weeks, and after some pipe work on my street the water ran cloudy for two days. "Technically fine" stopped being good enough.

Municipal water picks up a lot between the treatment plant and your glass:

  • Chlorine and chloramines. They keep water safe in the pipes, but they are also why your tap smells like a pool.
  • Sediment and rust, especially in buildings with old plumbing.
  • Lead. Still a real problem in homes with old pipes or lead service lines.
  • PFAS, the so called forever chemicals, showing up in more and more water supplies.
  • Hardness minerals like calcium and magnesium. They slowly kill kettles, dishwashers, and water heaters.
  • Trace contaminants nobody wants to think about: microplastics, pesticide runoff, pharmaceutical residues.

A filter is the last checkpoint before your glass. Worth getting right.

Reverse osmosis: the deep dive

RO is the most thorough drinking water purification you can install at home. It forces water through a semi permeable membrane with pores around 0.0001 microns. Small enough to block dissolved salts, lead, fluoride, nitrates, PFAS, and the vast majority of contaminants.

What the stages do

  1. Sediment pre filter. Catches sand, rust, and particles so the later stages don't clog.
  2. Carbon pre filter. Strips chlorine, which would otherwise damage the RO membrane.
  3. The RO membrane. The core of the system. Rejects dissolved contaminants.
  4. Polishing carbon filter. Final pass for taste.
  5. Remineralization (optional). Adds healthy minerals back and balances pH.

Specs that actually matter when comparing units

  • Membrane rating (GPD). Gallons per day. 50 GPD is plenty for most households; larger families should look at 75 to 100.
  • Stage count. 4 to 5 stages is the standard for a complete system.
  • Waste ratio. Older units wasted a lot of water. Modern high efficiency models are much better, check this if it matters to you.
  • NSF certifications. NSF/ANSI 42, 53, 58, and 401 mean an independent lab verified the claims. Marketing copy means nothing without these.
  • Filter cost and availability. Check replacement prices before you buy. This is where cheap units get expensive.

Buying guide: get this right in five steps

  1. Test your water first. Read your city's annual water quality report. On well water or in an old house, pay for an independent lab test. It costs 20 to 50 dollars and tells you exactly what you're dealing with.
  2. Match the system to the problem. Just chlorine taste? Carbon handles it. Lead, PFAS, or high TDS? You want reverse osmosis. Bacteria worries? Add UV.
  3. Size it for your household. A family of five burns through far more filtered water than someone living alone. Check daily production ratings.
  4. Price the maintenance, not just the unit. Look up replacement filter costs and intervals before you commit.
  5. Compare real units side by side. When you're ready, you can buy RO System models there and line up the specs. Ignore the marketing and compare stage count, membrane rating, waste ratio, and NSF certifications. That's what separates good units from mediocre ones.

Living with an RO system

Installation took me about an hour and a half under the kitchen sink. Most units come with decent instructions. If cutting into a water line makes you nervous, a plumber knocks it out in under two hours.

The difference is immediate. No chlorine smell. Coffee and tea taste noticeably better. The kettle stays clean.

Maintenance schedule

  • Every 6 to 12 months: replace sediment and carbon pre filters.
  • Every 12 months: replace the polishing filter.
  • Every 2 to 3 years: replace the RO membrane, depending on your incoming water quality.
  • Watch for signs: pressure drops, tastes or smells returning, or a TDS meter reading creeping up. Many newer units have filter life indicator lights that take the guesswork out.

Questions I kept asking while researching

Does RO strip out the good minerals too?

Yes, it takes most dissolved minerals along with the bad stuff. That's what the remineralization stage is for. Honestly though, for most people with a normal diet, drinking water is a minor source of minerals anyway.

Is RO water too pure to drink?

No. That's a myth that won't die. It's just very clean water. Remineralization helps with taste if you find plain RO flat, but there's nothing harmful about it.

Can I install it myself?

Most under sink kits are built for DIY with basic tools. If cutting into a water line makes you nervous, hire a plumber. It's a quick job for them.

Whole house or under sink, which first?

If your budget covers one, start under the sink. That's the water you drink and cook with, so it's where filtration matters most. Add whole house later if sediment or chlorine bothers you at every tap.

How do I know the system is still working?

A cheap TDS meter tells you in seconds. Test your filtered water every few months. If the reading climbs well above your baseline, something needs replacing.
